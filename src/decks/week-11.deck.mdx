## This week adds

Assembly: every gated shot cut into one sequence, carrying its sound and
captions, and exported to a spec rather than a default.

```notes
The queue produces shots; it does not produce an episode. Assembly is where
every shot that cleared the quality gate gets cut into one sequence, and this
lecture is entirely about the three things that turn a folder of approved
clips into a piece someone can actually watch: the assembly itself, the sound
and captions carried through it, and the delivery spec that gets it onto a
platform without the platform mangling it on the way in.
```

---

## Assembly is not re-litigating the gate

Assembly decides order, pacing, and where a cut lands against a beat. A gated
shot does not get re-judged on its own merits here.

```notes
A shot that passed the quality gate does not get re-judged during assembly on
its own merits; assembly only decides order, pacing, and where a cut lands
relative to a beat. Reopening a gate decision at this stage means the gate
wasn't doing its job earlier, and it's usually faster to flag that for next
time than to relitigate one shot now.
```

## Loudness is a standard, not a feeling

<p class="deck-figure">-23.0<span class="deck-figure__unit">LUFS, ±1.0 LU, true peak not above -1 dBTP</span></p>

EBU R 128's target, and the reason "sounds about right" is not a delivery
note: it varies with the speaker, the room, and the listener. The measurement
does not.

<p class="deck-source">Source: EBU R 128 v5.0 (Nov 2023) — https://tech.ebu.ch/publications/r128</p>

```notes
The recommendation's own wording is that "Programme Loudness Level shall be
normalised to a Target Level of -23.0 LUFS", with the ±1.0 LU tolerance
allowed where the target is "not achievable practically (for example, live
programmes)", and True Peak Level "shall not exceed -1 dBTP". The meter has to
be compliant with ITU-R BS.1770, currently at revision 5.

One caveat that matters for a sixty-to-ninety-second episode: R 128 does not
recommend using Loudness Range on programmes shorter than a minute. The target
and the true-peak ceiling still apply; the range figure is not meaningful at
that length.
```

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> Captions are a text version of the speech and non-speech audio information
> needed to understand the content
>
> <cite>W3C WAI, Captions/Subtitles</cite>

<p class="deck-source">Source: W3C WAI, Captions/Subtitles — https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/</p>

```notes
Note what the sentence includes: non-speech audio. A caption track that
carries the dialogue and nothing else has met half the definition. The door
slamming off screen, the rain that stops, the music cue that tells you the
scene has turned — if a viewer needs it to follow the episode, it belongs in
the captions.

Captions for prerecorded video with audio sit at WCAG Level A, which is the
lowest conformance level rather than the highest: it is the floor. Open
captions are burnt into the picture and travel wherever the file does; closed
captions ride alongside it and can be turned off, restyled, or translated.
Both count.
```

## Delivery is a spec, not a save button

A cut that looks correct on your own machine and fails a platform's check has
not shipped. It has failed at the most expensive point in the pipeline.

| Requirement       | YouTube's published recommendation |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Container         | MP4, moov atom first               |
| Video             | H.264 High Profile, closed GOP     |
| Audio             | AAC-LC, 48 kHz                     |
| 1080p SDR bitrate | 8 Mbps at 24-30 fps                |

<p class="deck-source">Source: YouTube Help, recommended upload encoding settings — https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1722171</p>

```notes
Exporting a finished cut means matching whatever a platform's delivery spec
actually asks for — resolution, frame rate, container format, caption format —
rather than exporting on default settings and hoping it's close enough.

Two lines on that page are worth more than the rest put together. Upload at
the frame rate the material was recorded at rather than converting it, and put
the moov atom at the front of the file, which is what lets playback start
before the whole file has arrived. Vertical needs no special handling on that
platform: the player adapts. Chroma subsampling is 4:2:0, and 48 to 60 fps at
1080p wants 12 Mbps rather than 8.
```

## The same cut, more than one door

![bg right:40%](./assets/cut-turn.avif)

TikTok's ad spec wants 9:16 at 540x960 or better, under 500MB, at 516 kbps or
above. Douyin's upload API caps a file at 128MB and 15 minutes and asks for
720x1280 vertical or better. One master, several exports.

<p class="deck-source">Source: TikTok Ads Manager, Video Ads Specifications — https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/video-ads-specifications<br />Source: 抖音开放平台, 上传视频 — https://open.douyin.com/platform/resource/docs/openapi/video-management/douyin/create/upload/</p>

```notes
Both of these are worth labelling honestly, because neither is the consumer
app's own rulebook. The first is an advertising spec, which is the strictest
published statement that platform makes about a vertical file. The second is
an open-platform upload API, which is what a publishing integration has to
satisfy rather than what a person dragging a file into the app experiences.

Read together they say something a single spec would not: the floors are low,
the ceilings differ, and the only file that satisfies all of them is one you
exported deliberately from a master you kept. Which is why the master is
1080x1920 in this course's own reference episode, and the web copies are
smaller.
```

## Sound and captions, carried through

A caption out of sync, or a line buried once music goes under it, is an
assembly failure even though no single shot caused it. And the target moves
with the door: Apple Podcasts asks for around -16 dB LKFS, not -23.

<p class="deck-source">Source: Apple Podcasts for Creators, Audio requirements — https://podcasters.apple.com/support/893-audio-requirements</p>

```notes
Dialogue clarity and captions are not a separate pass bolted on at the end;
they're properties every shot in the queue was already supposed to carry, and
assembly's job is making sure nothing got lost matching cuts against each
other. A caption that's present but out of sync, or a dialogue line that reads
clearly in isolation but gets buried once music is layered under it, is an
assembly failure even though no single shot caused it alone.

The second sentence is there to stop -23 becoming a superstition. Apple's
podcast target is around -16 dB LKFS with a ±1 dB tolerance, measured by the
same ITU-R BS.1770 method, and its true-peak ceiling is the same -1. Different
delivery, different target, same discipline: find the published number for the
door you are going through, and measure rather than listen.
```
