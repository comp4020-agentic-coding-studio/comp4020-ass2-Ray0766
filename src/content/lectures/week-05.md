Vertical drama is watched thumb-first: a viewer's default action is to keep
scrolling, and a shot has roughly three seconds to give them a reason not
to. Everything else this course teaches is wasted if the first three
seconds don't earn the rest of the episode, so this week is entirely about
the opening shot.

## Four kinds of hook

A cold open reliably uses one of four devices in its first shot: a mystery
hook, which shows a consequence without its cause and makes the viewer want
the missing setup; a conflict-in-progress hook, which starts mid-argument
or mid-chase rather than building up to it; a visual-anomaly hook, which
puts something wrong or unexpected in frame before any dialogue explains
it; and a direct-address hook, which has a character speak straight to
camera as if to the viewer. Pick one on purpose — a shot trying to be all
four at once earns none of them.

## Cutting the setup

The instinct to establish a location or a character before the story
starts is exactly backwards for this format: cut the establishing shot and
start inside the scene that would normally follow it. A viewer who needs
context will stay for it if the hook worked; a viewer who was never going
to stay was never going to sit through the establishing shot either.

## Beat rhythm

Inside the episode, attention is held the same way it was won: with a turn
every few seconds, and the cut itself landing on or just after that turn
rather than before it. A hook that isn't followed by beats at a similar
rhythm just delays the scroll instead of preventing it.

## Before class

## This week's exercise
