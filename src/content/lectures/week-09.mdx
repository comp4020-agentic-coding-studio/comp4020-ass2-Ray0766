Phase 3 has covered three separate ways of holding a shot steady: your
own workflow, a locked reference, and a scene master plate. This week is
where those three get organised into something long enough to call an
episode — a beat sheet, a hook, and a storyboard that turns both into a
shot list the production phase can actually run.

## States, not transitions

A beat sheet works best written as a sequence of states — what is true
at this point in the story — rather than a sequence of transitions
describing how one moment turns into the next. "The rival arrives
uninvited" is a state; "then things get tense" is a transition
describing a feeling rather than naming anything a shot could show. A
beat sheet written entirely in states gives a storyboard something
concrete to draw; one written in transitions leaves the storyboard
guessing at what actually needs to be on screen.

## The three-second hook

A vertical episode is watched thumb-first, and a viewer's default action
is to keep scrolling. TikTok publishes two windows for the opening of a
video on its own feed: "Prioritize your hook in the first 6 seconds to
boost engagement and increase watch time", and "Introduce your content
proposition in the first 3 seconds for better recall and awareness". Two
windows, not one — and the inner one is the one this course plans to,
which is what "the three-second hook" means here. Not that the hook is
finished by then, but that by then the opening has said what it is.

Both numbers come with conditions. This is advertising guidance about a
feed, not a finding about drama — though the feed is where a vertical
episode lands either way. And a published window is not a target:
nothing good happens because a hook finished at 2.9 seconds. What the
two numbers settle is the order of business, which is the part worth
keeping — the proposition first, then the rest of the hook.

This course sorts cold opens into four devices — its own sorting, not a
published taxonomy: a mystery hook showing a consequence without its
cause, a conflict-in-progress hook starting mid-argument, a
visual-anomaly hook putting something wrong in frame before anything
explains it, and a direct-address hook speaking straight to camera. Pick
one on purpose — a shot trying to be all four earns none of them, and
the hook is the first state on the beat sheet, not an afterthought
bolted onto it.

[The Cut](/cut/) makes this move on four real recorded clips: drop the
establishing shot, open on the hook instead, and watch what actually
changes.

## The storyboard as a shot list

A storyboard for this course is not a drawing exercise; it's a shot
list with a size and a reference attached to each entry — which
reference sheet or master plate that shot checks against, and roughly
how close the frame is. A board that only shows composition without
naming its reference is a set of pictures, not a production document;
whoever shoots from it later needs to know which locked asset each
panel is supposed to match.

## The ladder

The ladder this week runs four tiers, each covering the same
two-to-three-shot sequence. The lowest tier is a prose paragraph
describing what happens, with no shot breakdown at all. The next tier
turns that paragraph into a beat sheet written in states. The next tier
adds a shot list to the beat sheet, with a size named for every shot.
The top tier boards the sequence with actual keyframes, each one tagged
with the reference material it has to match.
