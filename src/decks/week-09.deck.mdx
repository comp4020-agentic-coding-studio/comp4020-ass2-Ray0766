## States, not transitions

"The rival arrives uninvited" is a state a shot can show. "Then things get
tense" is a feeling, and leaves the storyboard guessing.

<LadderStill week={9} tier="t2" />

```notes
A beat sheet works best written as a sequence of states — what is true at this
point in the story — rather than a sequence of transitions describing how one
moment turns into the next. "The rival arrives uninvited" is a state; "then
things get tense" is a transition describing a feeling rather than naming
anything a shot could show. A beat sheet written entirely in states gives a
storyboard something concrete to draw; one written in transitions leaves the
storyboard guessing at what actually needs to be on screen.

The recorded beat sheet is written this way and it is worth reading for the
result lines: "Result state: Heracles is down in the water, his right hand on
a torch stake at the shore." That sentence can be drawn. "The fight turns"
cannot.
```

## The opening is a published window

<p class="deck-figure">3<span class="deck-figure__unit">seconds to introduce the content proposition</span></p>

A vertical episode is watched thumb-first, and the default action is to keep
scrolling. TikTok's own creative guidance puts the hook in the first six
seconds and the proposition inside the first three.

<p class="deck-source">Source: TikTok Ads Manager Help, creative best practices — https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices</p>

```notes
The same page is worth reading for the rest of its list, because it is a
platform describing its own surface rather than a commentator guessing at it:
shoot vertical at 9:16 and at least 720p, and keep text overlays to five to
ten words per second so a caption can actually be read at the pace the cut
runs.

Two things to hold on to. This is advertising guidance, so read it as a
statement about the feed rather than about drama — but the feed is where a
vertical episode lands either way. And a number like this is a window, not a
target: nothing good happens because a hook finished at 2.9 seconds.
```

```notes
Worth keeping the form's own boundary in view while writing to it: YouTube
defines Shorts as "short-form videos that are up to 3 minutes long", so the
hook is not competing against a feature film's patience. It is competing
against the next thumb movement.
```

## The storyboard is a shot list

![bg right:40%](./assets/week09-t3.avif)

The recorded tier three names a size, a duration and a camera for every entry.
S8, 0.0 to 4.0 s, medium, slow push-in. S9, 4.0 to 7.0 s, close-up on the hand
and the stake. S10, 7.0 to 12.0 s, medium side profile, static.

<p class="deck-source">Source, for the shot-list vocabulary: Bordwell, Thompson & Smith, Film Art, 13th ed. — https://www.mheducation.com/highered/product/film-art-introduction-bordwell-thompson/M9781264296095.html<br />Source, for the shot numbers and timings: this course's own recorded run — src/data/studio/inputs/week09/t3.md</p>

```notes
Two different kinds of claim were sitting under one citation. Size, angle and
cut are the textbook's vocabulary and it is the right book for them. S8, S9,
S10 and the 4.0 and 7.0 second marks are not in that book and could not be:
they are read off this week's own recorded shot list, which says "S9 4.0–7.0 s
CLOSE-UP on the hand and the stake, slight low angle, static camera." A
publisher's product page cannot support a number, and pointing at one for
these was asking it to.

A storyboard for this course is not a drawing exercise; it's a shot list with
a size and a reference attached to each entry — which reference sheet or
master plate that shot checks against, and roughly how close the frame is. A
board that only shows composition without naming its reference is a set of
pictures, not a production document; whoever shoots from it later needs to
know which locked asset each panel is supposed to match.

The shot-scale vocabulary — long shot, medium, close-up, and the rest — is not
this course's invention and is worth learning from the standard reference
rather than from a prompt guide. Film Art's three chapters on the shot and on
editing are where the terms this deck uses come from, and they are the terms a
crew will assume you mean.
```

## Pick one device on purpose

Mystery, conflict in progress, visual anomaly, or direct address. A shot
trying to be all four earns none of them.

<LadderStill week={9} tier="t4" />

```notes
This course sorts cold opens into four devices — its own sorting, not a
published taxonomy: a mystery hook showing a consequence without its cause, a
conflict-in-progress hook starting mid-argument, a visual-anomaly hook putting
something wrong in frame before anything explains it, and a direct-address
hook speaking straight to camera. Pick one on purpose — a shot trying to be
all four earns none of them, and the hook is the first state on the beat
sheet, not an afterthought bolted onto it.
```

## The ladder

The same two-to-three-shot sequence, four ways.

1. A prose paragraph, with no shot breakdown at all
2. That paragraph as a beat sheet written in states
3. Plus a shot list, with a size named for every shot
4. Boarded with keyframes, each tagged to the reference it must match

```notes
The ladder this week runs four tiers, each covering the same
two-to-three-shot sequence. The lowest tier is a prose paragraph describing
what happens, with no shot breakdown at all. The next tier turns that
paragraph into a beat sheet written in states. The next tier adds a shot list
to the beat sheet, with a size named for every shot. The top tier boards the
sequence with actual keyframes, each one tagged with the reference material it
has to match.
```
