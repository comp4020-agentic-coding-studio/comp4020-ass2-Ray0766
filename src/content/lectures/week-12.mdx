The pilot is finished and delivered; this lecture is about the five
minutes after someone presses play, and about everything past that
first episode — a second one, a season, a feature cut. None of that is
a bigger version of the same problem. Some of it scales cleanly from
what the semester has already built, and some of it does not scale at
all.

## Reading the curve

A retention curve plots what share of viewers are still watching at
each point in an episode, and its shape matters more than its overall
average: a steady curve with one sharp drop tells a different story
than a slow, even decline, even where both end at the same final
number. The sharp drop is where something specific went wrong; the slow
decline is closer to the whole episode being paced too loosely
throughout.

<RetentionCurve />

Every drop-off point maps back to a specific decision earlier in the
process — a hook that didn't land in its first three seconds, a cut
that read as confusing rather than deliberate, a stretch where the beat
sheet's density flattened out. The curve doesn't explain why a moment
failed; it only points at where to look.

## The bible carries forward what the pilot built

A series bible is the document that lets a second episode start from
where the first one left off rather than from zero: the model sheets
and master plates that lock identity, the beat-sheet conventions that
worked, the hook types that held an audience and the ones that didn't.
Writing the bible once, after the pilot, is what makes a second episode
cheaper to produce than the first one — without it, every new episode
re-solves continuity and pacing problems the pilot already solved.

## The release calendar

A season is not just a pile of finished episodes; it's a schedule
someone commits to and a viewer can rely on. A release calendar states
when each episode goes out and holds that date the same way an
assessment's due date gets held — a season that ships on an
unpredictable schedule loses the audience a retention curve is trying
to measure in the first place, regardless of how good any single
episode is.

## What scales, and what doesn't

Generation, continuity discipline, and the quality gate scale close to
linearly: more episodes need more passes through the same queue, with
the same roles, checked against the same reference material. What
doesn't scale the same way is judgment — deciding which hook actually
fits a beat, deciding whether a drop on the curve is a pacing problem
or a one-off, deciding what belongs in the bible and what was a
one-episode accident. Adding more people to a queue multiplies
throughput; it does not multiply the judgment behind any one decision,
which stays a single person's job no matter how many episodes are in
production.

## Reading

- [YouTube Help: measure key moments for audience retention](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415)
  — the curve shapes the platform actually reports, the baselines it
  compares a video against, and the vocabulary it has retired since the
  readings most people learned.
- [WGA 2020 Theatrical and Television Basic Agreement](https://www.wga.org/uploadedfiles/contracts/mba20.pdf)
  — article 13.B.7.m defines a bible as a written document carrying the
  format, character descriptions and story lines for a number of
  episodes, which is the standard yours is held to.
- [NRTA CVB: 2025 satellite-channel micro-drama viewing report](https://www.nrta.gov.cn/art/2025/12/26/art_114_72199.html)
  — a year of the form on broadcast television, which is the clearest
  published measure of it scaling past the feed it started in.
