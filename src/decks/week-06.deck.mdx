## This week adds

Image to video: a finished keyframe locks the shot, and the prompt's only
remaining job is to say what happens next.

```notes
Image to video reverses the order of the last two weeks: rather than
describing a subject in words and hoping the camera cooperates, a finished
keyframe locks composition, framing, and art direction first, and the prompt's
only remaining job is to say what happens next. This is the last of the four
generators, and the strongest control the semester offers over a single shot —
which is exactly why the phase ends here.
```

## Keyframe-first control

The keyframe fixes who is in frame, how they are dressed, where the light
falls. The motion prompt only has to describe change.

<LadderStill week={6} tier="t3" />

```notes
Starting from a keyframe fixes everything text to video leaves loose: who's in
frame, how they're dressed, where the light falls, what the background looks
like. The motion prompt that follows only has to describe change — what moves,
and how — because everything that isn't supposed to change is already locked
in the frame the model was given.
```

## One frame in, a fixed number of frames out

<p class="deck-figure">25<span class="deck-figure__unit">frames at 576 x 1024, from one context frame</span></p>

Stable Video Diffusion's published shape for a single still. The base model
generates fourteen; the extended one generates twenty-five, at the same
resolution as the frame it was given.

<p class="deck-source">Source: stable-video-diffusion-img2vid-xt model card — https://huggingface.co/stabilityai/stable-video-diffusion-img2vid-xt</p>

```notes
The card's wording is exact: SVD-XT is "trained to generate 25 frames at
resolution 576x1024 given a context frame of the same size", where the base
SVD does 14. Two things follow. The output resolution is the input
resolution — the keyframe is not a suggestion about composition, it is the
canvas — and the clip length is a property of the checkpoint rather than
something the prompt negotiates.

The paper behind it describes three training stages, quoted on the next slide,
and that structure is why an image-to-video model behaves like an image model
that learned to move rather than a video model that learned to draw.
```

```notes
Three stages, in that order, and the order is the argument: the model learns
to make a picture first, then learns what pictures do over time, then gets
polished on a small clean set. It is the same ramp this phase runs on, which
is not a coincidence so much as the same constraint showing up twice — motion
is easier to learn on top of a settled image than alongside one.
```

## First and last frame

![bg right:40%](./assets/week06-t5.avif)

<LadderInput
  week={6}

Two frames constrain motion far more tightly than one frame and a prompt.
The node takes `start_image`, `end_image` and a `length` defaulting to 81;
H3's FL2VA accepts zero, one, or two images.

<p class="deck-source">Source: ComfyUI WanFirstLastFrameToVideo — https://docs.comfy.org/built-in-nodes/WanFirstLastFrameToVideo<br />Source: MiniMax-H3 card — https://huggingface.co/MiniMaxAI/MiniMax-H3</p>

```notes
Some workflows accept two keyframes instead of one: a starting frame and an
ending frame, with the model generating everything in between. That constrains
motion far more tightly than a first frame and a text prompt alone, at the
cost of needing a second finished keyframe to already exist before the shot
can be generated — a trade worth making when the ending pose matters as much
as the starting one.

The recorded tier five is exactly this, and the prompt names the alignment in
so many words: Picture 1 aligns with the 0.00-second mark and Picture 2 with
the 5.17-second mark. Wan2.1's first-last-frame model carries a restriction
worth knowing before you plan around it — "Currently, only 720P is supported".
```

## Motion versus identity

When a take drifts, the fix is a calmer motion prompt, not a stronger one.
VBench scores the two separately: Subject Consistency from DINO feature
similarity across frames, Background Consistency from CLIP feature similarity,
Temporal Flickering from mean absolute difference.

<p class="deck-source">Source: VBench — https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17982</p>

```notes
The risk specific to this generator is a shot that drifts identity while
chasing motion: a face or costume detail changing partway through the clip
because the motion prompt asked for more movement than the model could deliver
while holding the keyframe's details steady. When a take drifts, the fix is
almost never a stronger motion prompt — it's a calmer one, asking for less
change per second so identity has room to hold.

VBench is useful here less as a leaderboard than as a vocabulary: it splits
what a viewer calls "it looks wrong" into sixteen dimensions, and the four
that matter to this week are the three named on the slide plus Motion
Smoothness. Dynamic Degree sits opposite them — a clip that holds perfectly
still scores well on consistency and badly on movement, which is the trade
this slide is about.
```

## The ladder

1. A rough sketch as the starting frame
2. A flat, unlit keyframe
3. A finished keyframe with lighting resolved
4. That keyframe plus a motion prompt written as the four sentences
5. Plus a last frame, or a small reference set, to hold identity

```notes
The ladder for image to video runs five tiers, climbing through how finished
the starting keyframe is and how much the motion prompt is asked to do. The
lowest tier starts from a rough sketch. The next tier starts from a flat,
unlit keyframe. The next tier starts from a finished keyframe with lighting
resolved. The next tier keeps that finished keyframe and adds a motion prompt
written as the four sentences from earlier in the phase. The top tier keeps
all of that and adds either a last frame or a small reference set to hold
identity through more motion than a single keyframe alone can guarantee.
```
