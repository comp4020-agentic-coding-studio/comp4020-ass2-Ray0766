Everything before this week has been one shot at a time. A Longer Cut asks
for several scenes in sequence, which moves the unit of craft from the shot
to the join between shots — and between scenes, the join is narrative as
much as it is technical.

## Scene-to-scene grammar

Cutting from one scene to the next needs its own signal, distinct from a cut
inside a scene: a change of location, a time jump, or a shift in who is on
screen, marked clearly enough that a viewer doesn't mistake it for a
continuity error. The tools are ordinary — an establishing beat, a sound
bridge, a match on action or color — but they have to be chosen on purpose,
because a generator produces isolated clips with no sense of what came
before or after.

## Shoot coverage, decide later

Coverage thinking means generating more material than any one cut will use —
a second angle, a slower version of a beat, an alternate reaction — and
making the rhythm decision in the edit rather than committing to it at
generation time. One generated take is a proposal, not a final answer; a
season's pacing comes from choosing among several, not from getting one take
right on the first pass.

## Retention past the hook

Week 5's three-second hook only buys the opening. Past that, a mid-length cut
still has to keep earning attention, and the way it does that is beat
rhythm: density can ease off after the opening, but it cannot flatten into a
stretch where nothing happens, or the piece loses its viewer exactly where
the hook stops carrying it.

## Sound carries the join

A sound bridge — audio from the next scene starting before its picture
arrives, or the previous scene's sound trailing into the cut — smooths a
scene change that would otherwise read as an abrupt jump, and costs nothing
to try against a hard cut in the edit.
