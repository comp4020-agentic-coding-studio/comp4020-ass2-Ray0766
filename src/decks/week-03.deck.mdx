## This week adds

Text to image: a prompt that reliably produces one still frame good enough to
build the rest of the week on.

```notes
Text to image is the first of four generators this course teaches, and it
produces the smallest possible unit of work: one still frame, called a
keyframe once it's good enough to build something else on top of it.
Everything from here through the phase either generates a keyframe or does
something to one, so a prompt that reliably produces a usable keyframe is this
week's entire craft question.
```

## Why anime, first

<p class="deck-figure">8 million<span class="deck-figure__unit">tagged images in the caption corpus</span></p>

Flat colour and clean linework absorb a generator's small errors as line
style. They also arrive with a vocabulary: Illustrious reports training on
Danbooru's corpus of over eight million images, so the checkpoint answers in
that corpus's own words.

<p class="deck-source">Source: Illustrious: an Open Advanced Illustration Model — https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19946</p>

```notes
This phase works in anime grammar on purpose, and it's a technical decision,
not an aesthetic one. A generator's small mistakes — a slightly wrong
proportion, an edge that drifts — read differently depending on the style they
land in: flat colour and clean linework compress detail into shape and
outline, so a minor error gets absorbed as "that's the line style." The same
magnitude of error on a photorealistic face lands squarely in the range human
perception is most sensitive to and reads as wrong rather than stylised.

The second reason is the one on this slide. Illustrious builds on the SDXL
family — a "three times larger UNet backbone" than the previous generation,
with a second text encoder, OpenCLIP ViT-bigG alongside CLIP ViT-L — and
trains it on a tag-captioned dataset rather than a prose-captioned one. That
is why the vocabulary matters more here than the sentence does.
```

---

## Four things a keyframe prompt should say

Subject. Framing — size and angle. What the light is doing. A style anchor
naming the checkpoint's own register. In that order.

```notes
A usable prompt for a still collapses into four decisions, each earning
roughly one clause: who or what the subject is, how the shot is framed — size
and angle — what the light is doing, and a style anchor naming the
checkpoint's own aesthetic register rather than leaving it to chance. Write
those four in order and the prompt is usable before any further polish gets
added. The recorded ladder's middle tier is written exactly this way, as four
labelled lines and nothing else.
```

## The checkpoint publishes its own settings

An anime checkpoint is not a general-purpose model with a style applied. Its
card states the numbers it was tuned for, and they are not the defaults.

| Setting             | NoobAI-XL 1.1 |
| ------------------- | ------------- |
| CFG                 | 5 to 6        |
| Steps               | 25 to 30      |
| Sampler             | Euler a       |
| Vertical resolution | 768 x 1344    |

<p class="deck-source">Source: NoobAI-XL 1.1 model card — https://huggingface.co/Laxhar/noobai-XL-1.1</p>

```notes
Four numbers, all off the model card, and all lower than the generic defaults
from last week: CFG 5 to 6 against 8.0, steps 25 to 30 against 20. A card that
publishes its own settings is telling you the generic ones will cost you
rolls.

The last row is a generation size and not a delivery frame, and the two are
different things worth keeping apart: 768 x 1344 is 4:7, or 0.5714, while the
9:16 the episode ships in is 0.5625. What the card publishes is a shape the
model was trained to produce, so asking it for a true 9:16 instead buys worse
rolls, not a better frame. Meeting the delivery frame is a crop or a scale at
the end of the pipeline, in week 11.

The same card is where the licence complication for this family lives. Its
metadata declares `fair-ai-public-license-1.0-sd`, and the README adds a
sentence of its own: "We prohibit any form of commercialization." Those are
two separate instruments and the second is not part of the first, so a shot
built on this checkpoint has to satisfy both.
```

{/* _class: quote */}

> person count ||| character names ||| rating ||| general tags ||| artist |||
> score range based rating ||| year modifier
>
> <cite>Illustrious, on caption order</cite>

<p class="deck-source">Source: Illustrious: an Open Advanced Illustration Model — https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19946</p>

```notes
This is the order the captions were written in during training, which is the
order the model learned to read a prompt in. It is not a rule you can be
graded against, but it explains why moving a tag changes a result at all, and
why a checkpoint answers a tag list more precisely than a sentence saying the
same thing.

Danbooru's own community wiki is where the tag categories come from: Artist,
Character, Copyright — which "identify the anime, manga, game, or novel" —
General, which "objectively describe the contents", and Meta. Spaces in tag
names become underscores. The quality tiers Illustrious describes run from
"Worst quality ~8%" up through "Best quality ~92%, Masterpiece ~100%", which
is why "masterpiece" is a percentile and not a compliment.
```

## The checkpoint's own vocabulary

![bg right:40%](./assets/week03-t4.avif)

Tier four opens `masterpiece, best quality, amazing quality, very aesthetic,
absurdres, newest` and its negative opens `worst quality, low quality,
lowres, bad hands`. NoobAI-XL's card recommends almost exactly that pair.
These are trained tokens, not adjectives.

<p class="deck-source">Source: NoobAI-XL 1.1 model card — https://huggingface.co/Laxhar/noobai-XL-1.1</p>

```notes
Anime-trained checkpoints are largely trained on tag-style captions, not
prose, so they respond more precisely to a checkpoint's own tag vocabulary
than to a naturally written sentence saying the same thing. Pairing that
vocabulary with a negative prompt — naming what to suppress, not just what to
include — removes far more bad rolls than adding qualifiers to the positive
prompt ever does.

The card's own recommended positive is "masterpiece, best quality, newest,
absurdres, highres, safe," and its negative is "nsfw, worst quality, old,
early, low quality, lowres, signature, username, logo, bad hands, mutated
hands". The recorded ladder's tier four is that pair with the shot's own tags
threaded through it. The card also documents the year tags — newest is
2021–2024, old is 2005–2010 — which is a date filter wearing a style tag's
clothes.
```

## The ladder

1. One line naming the subject alone
2. Plus a line of setting and mood
3. The full four-part grammar — subject, framing, light, style anchor
4. Plus the checkpoint's tag vocabulary and a negative prompt
5. Plus shot size, camera angle, and a colour script for the sequence

```notes
The ladder for a text-to-image keyframe runs five tiers. The lowest tier is
one line naming the subject alone. The next tier adds a line of setting and
mood. The middle tier writes the full four-part grammar — subject, framing,
light, and a style anchor. The next tier keeps that grammar and adds the
checkpoint's own tag vocabulary plus a negative prompt. The top tier keeps
everything below it and adds a specific shot size, camera angle, and a colour
script naming the palette across the sequence the keyframe belongs to.
```
