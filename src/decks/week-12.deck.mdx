## Reading the curve

Shape matters more than the average. A steady curve with one sharp drop tells
a different story than a slow, even decline ending at the same number.

```notes
A retention curve plots what share of viewers are still watching at each
point in an episode, and its shape matters more than its overall average: a
steady curve with one sharp drop tells a different story than a slow, even
decline, even where both end at the same final number. The sharp drop is
where something specific went wrong; the slow decline is closer to the whole
episode being paced too loosely throughout.
```

---

## The curve points, it does not explain

Every drop maps back to a decision earlier in the process. The curve says
where to look, never why the moment failed.

```notes
Every drop-off point maps back to a specific decision earlier in the process
— a hook that didn't land in its first three seconds, a cut that read as
confusing rather than deliberate, a stretch where the beat sheet's density
flattened out. The curve doesn't explain why a moment failed; it only points
at where to look.
```

---

## The bible carries forward what the pilot built

Model sheets, master plates, the beat-sheet conventions that worked, the
hooks that held. Written once, it makes the second episode cheaper.

```notes
A series bible is the document that lets a second episode start from where
the first one left off rather than from zero: the model sheets and master
plates that lock identity, the beat-sheet conventions that worked, the hook
types that held an audience and the ones that didn't. Writing the bible once,
after the pilot, is what makes a second episode cheaper to produce than the
first one — without it, every new episode re-solves continuity and pacing
problems the pilot already solved.
```

---

## The release calendar

A season is a schedule someone commits to and a viewer can rely on, held the
same way an assessment's due date is held.

```notes
A season is not just a pile of finished episodes; it's a schedule someone
commits to and a viewer can rely on. A release calendar states when each
episode goes out and holds that date the same way an assessment's due date
gets held — a season that ships on an unpredictable schedule loses the
audience a retention curve is trying to measure in the first place,
regardless of how good any single episode is.
```

---

## What scales, and what doesn't

The queue, continuity discipline and the gate scale close to linearly.
Judgment does not: it stays one person's job however many episodes run.

```notes
Generation, continuity discipline, and the quality gate scale close to
linearly: more episodes need more passes through the same queue, with the
same roles, checked against the same reference material. What doesn't scale
the same way is judgment — deciding which hook actually fits a beat, deciding
whether a drop on the curve is a pacing problem or a one-off, deciding what
belongs in the bible and what was a one-episode accident. Adding more people
to a queue multiplies throughput; it does not multiply the judgment behind
any one decision, which stays a single person's job no matter how many
episodes are in production.
```
