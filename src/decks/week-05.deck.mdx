## This week adds

Text to video: motion generated from words alone, with the prompt as the only
thing anchoring the shot.

```notes
Text to video generates motion from words alone, with nothing to anchor
composition but the prompt itself. That's weaker control than starting from a
keyframe, but it's the fastest way to test an idea before committing an image
to it, and every video generator this course uses has a native clip length
that the prompt has to respect rather than fight.
```

## The native clip length is a published number

It is not a style choice and not a limit to argue with. It is in the config
file, and the prompt is written inside it.

| Model                | Native clip                    |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Wan2.1 shared config | 81 frames at 16 fps — 5.06 s   |
| Wan2.2 TI2V-5B       | 121 frames at 24 fps           |
| MiniMax H3           | 4 to 15 s, at 24 fps           |

<p class="deck-source">Source: Wan configs, `shared_config.py` and `wan_ti2v_5B.py` — https://github.com/Wan-Video/Wan2.2<br />Source: MiniMax-H3 model card, Output — https://huggingface.co/MiniMaxAI/MiniMax-H3</p>

```notes
Every video generator this course uses has a native clip length — the duration
one pass reliably holds together before coherence starts to fall apart — that
the prompt has to respect rather than fight.

Two of these three numbers are not in a README; they are in a config file
inside the repository, which is worth saying out loud, because "how long is a
clip" is exactly the kind of question a project answers in code and nowhere
else. The H3 card also fixes the canvas: "shorter side is set to 768 pixels by
default", with 9:16 among the listed aspect ratios. Wan2.1's own README puts a
render cost beside its length: "a 5-second 480P video on an RTX 4090 in about
4 minutes", and the 1.3B model needs "only 8.19 GB VRAM".
```

```notes
Six words, and they explain the clip length better than any tip does. A video
model of this kind cuts the clip into patches across space and time and treats
each one as a token, the way a language model treats a word — so a longer clip
is more tokens, and coherence across the whole clip is a property of how many
of them the model can hold in relation to each other. The same report says
plainly that "Sora is a diffusion transformer", and the architecture behind
that name replaced "the commonly-used U-Net backbone with a transformer that
operates on latent patches", with the paper reporting that higher Gflops
"consistently have lower FID".
```

## The prompt has fields, not paragraphs

![bg right:40%](./assets/week05-t3.avif)

The recorded ladder's middle tier is written into three:
`integrated_multimodal_description` carries visuals, actions, shots, speakers
and dialogue along the timeline; `overall_soundscape` takes one to four
English sentences; `non_diegetic_music` is the music the characters cannot
hear.

<p class="deck-source">Source: MiniMax H3 Video Prompt Writing Guide — https://huggingface.co/MiniMaxAI/MiniMax-H3/raw/main/docs/VIDEO_PROMPT_WRITING_GUIDE_base_en.md</p>

```notes
A usable video prompt collapses into four sentences, each doing one job: who
or what the subject is and what it's doing, how close the shot is — the shot
size, from wide to close-up — what the camera itself is doing while the
subject acts, and what the light is doing. Write those four in order, inside
the model's native clip length, and the prompt is usable before style ever
enters the conversation.

Where those four sentences go is the thing this slide adds. The guide splits
the prompt into named fields, and sound is not an afterthought in it: the
soundscape is its own field, and music the characters cannot hear is a third
field again, kept apart from the diegetic audio described along the timeline.
A prompt written as one undifferentiated paragraph is throwing that structure
away.
```

---

## The four sentences

Subject and action. Shot size. What the camera is doing. What the light is
doing. In that order, inside the clip length.

```notes
Four sentences, four jobs, and the order matters because each one narrows the
last: the subject decides what is in frame, the shot size decides how much of
it we see, the camera decides how that changes over the clip, and the light
decides what it looks like while it happens. Style is not one of the four. It
goes in once these are settled, or it competes with them.
```

## Reading a bad take

Wrong physics, a quiet change of subject partway through, or a camera move
that contradicts the prompt. Each points at a different sentence.

<LadderStill week={5} tier="t5" />

```notes
A failed text-to-video take tends to fail in one of three specific ways:
motion that reads as physically wrong rather than merely stylised, a cut where
the model quietly changes what the subject looks like partway through the
clip, or a camera move that contradicts what the prompt asked for. Naming
which of the three happened is more useful than a general "this doesn't look
right," because each one points at a different sentence in the prompt to fix.

The still here is the ladder's counter-example rather than its top rung: the
same four sentences buried under contradictory camera and style demands, which
is what over-specifying costs.
```

## The ladder

1. One line naming only the subject
2. Plus the subject's action
3. The full four sentences — subject, shot size, camera, light
4. Plus a specific beat the action turns on, and a negative prompt

And a counter-example, not a fifth rung: the same four sentences buried under
contradictory camera and style demands, generated once on purpose.

```notes
The ladder for a text-to-video prompt runs four rising tiers, plus one tier
that is not a top but a warning. The lowest tier is one line naming only the
subject. The next tier adds the subject's action. The next tier writes out the
full four sentences — subject, shot size, camera, light. The top tier keeps
those four and adds a specific beat the action turns on, plus a negative
prompt naming what the shot must not do. And a counter-example, not a fifth
rung: the same four sentences buried under contradictory camera and style
demands at once, which reliably produces a worse result than the plain four
sentences alone — worth generating once, on purpose, to see what
over-specifying actually costs.
```
