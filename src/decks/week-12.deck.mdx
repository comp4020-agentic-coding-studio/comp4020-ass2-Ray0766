## Reading the curve

Shape matters more than the average. A steady curve with one sharp drop tells
a different story than a slow, even decline ending at the same number.

```notes
A retention curve plots what share of viewers are still watching at each point
in an episode, and its shape matters more than its overall average: a steady
curve with one sharp drop tells a different story than a slow, even decline,
even where both end at the same final number. The sharp drop is where
something specific went wrong; the slow decline is closer to the whole episode
being paced too loosely throughout.
```

## What the platform actually counts

The curve is not one measurement. Each of these answers a different question,
and only the first is about the opening.

| Metric               | What it counts                                  |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Intro                | the share still watching after the first 30 s   |
| Average view duration| average minutes watched, among engaged views     |

<p class="deck-source">Source: YouTube Help, Measure key moments for audience retention — https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415<br />Source: YouTube Help, Understand your YouTube engagement — https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9313698</p>

```notes
Two more things that page names and this table does not have room for. Top
moments are the moments where almost no one dropped off, which is a different
and more useful signal than the average. And a video is compared against
others of similar length as well as against your own ten most recent, so
"typical retention" is a moving baseline rather than a fixed bar.

The other platform on this course's list measures the same shape with
different words: TikTok's analytics report average watch time and the
percentage of viewers who watched the entire video, alongside where the
traffic came from — the For You feed, most of it.
```

## A season's money arrives early

<p class="deck-figure">80%<span class="deck-figure__unit">of total revenue, in the first month</span></p>

Reported for Douyin's own top-performing native micro-dramas, not for the
market as a whole: 80% in month one, 15% across months two and three, 5%
across four to six. A release calendar is not an administrative preference.

<p class="deck-source">Source: NRTA reprint of Xinhua, 微短剧迎来新变化 (Feb 2025) — https://www.nrta.gov.cn/art/2025/2/28/art_3731_70276.html</p>

```notes
The scope is in the sentence itself and is narrower than "the market": "抖音原生
头部微短剧的首月收入占总收入的80%，2-3月占比为15%，即使进入4-6月阶段，仍能保持5%的
收入占比。" Douyin-native, top tier, on one platform. The shape of the curve is
the transferable part; the exact percentages are not a number to quote about a
show on any other platform, and certainly not about one made in this course.

A season is not just a pile of finished episodes; it's a schedule someone
commits to and a viewer can rely on. A release calendar states when each
episode goes out and holds that date the same way an assessment's due date
gets held — a season that ships on an unpredictable schedule loses the
audience a retention curve is trying to measure in the first place, regardless
of how good any single episode is.

This slide is why. If four fifths of what a title earns arrives in its first
month, the calendar is not paperwork around the work: it is most of the
commercial decision, and a slipped episode is not a slipped episode, it is a
slipped window. The same report notes the free, ad-funded model at about half
the market by October 2024; the 2025 white paper puts that share at about
66.3%, which is the direction rather than a contradiction.
```

{/* _class: quote */}

> A "bible" is a written document containing the format, character
> descriptions and story lines or synopses for a number of episodes of a
> series.
>
> <cite>WGA 2020 Basic Agreement, Art. 13.B.7.m</cite>

<p class="deck-source">Source: WGA 2020 Theatrical and Television Basic Agreement — https://www.wga.org/uploadedfiles/contracts/mba20.pdf</p>

```notes
The bible is not a course exercise dressed up with a name. It is defined in a
collective bargaining agreement, because it is a deliverable someone gets paid
for — which tells you what standard it is held to: specific enough that a
second writer can work from it without asking the first one anything.

For this course it carries what the pilot built: the model sheets and master
plates that lock identity, the beat-sheet conventions that worked, the hook
types that held an audience and the ones that didn't. Writing it once, after
the pilot, is what makes a second episode cheaper than the first. Without it,
every new episode re-solves continuity and pacing problems the pilot already
solved.
```

## The curve points, it does not explain

![bg right:40%](./assets/reference-episode.avif)

Every drop maps back to a decision made earlier. The platform names the shapes
it reports — flat lines, gradual declines, spikes and dips — and it compares a
video against others of similar length. It never says why a moment failed.

<p class="deck-source">Source: YouTube Help, Measure key moments for audience retention — https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415</p>

```notes
Every drop-off point maps back to a specific decision earlier in the process —
a hook that didn't land in its first three seconds, a cut that read as
confusing rather than deliberate, a stretch where the beat sheet's density
flattened out. The curve doesn't explain why a moment failed; it only points
at where to look.

Which is also a warning about vocabulary. The wording on that page changes:
the terms "absolute retention" and "relative retention" have been retired, and
a reading written against them is now a reading of something the platform no
longer reports. Read the current page before you read your own curve.
```

## What scales, and what doesn't

The queue, continuity discipline and the gate scale close to linearly.
Judgment does not. The form scales too: 124 micro-drama titles ran on
satellite channels in 2025, up 254.3%, across 36 channels rather than ten.

<p class="deck-source">Source: NRTA CVB, 2025年全国卫视频道微短剧收视报告 (Dec 2025) — https://www.nrta.gov.cn/art/2025/12/26/art_114_72199.html</p>

```notes
Generation, continuity discipline, and the quality gate scale close to
linearly: more episodes need more passes through the same queue, with the same
roles, checked against the same reference material. What doesn't scale the
same way is judgment — deciding which hook actually fits a beat, deciding
whether a drop on the curve is a pacing problem or a one-off, deciding what
belongs in the bible and what was a one-episode accident. Adding more people
to a queue multiplies throughput; it does not multiply the judgment behind any
one decision, which stays a single person's job no matter how many episodes
are in production.

The number on the slide is the form arriving somewhere it did not used to be:
broadcast television. Cumulative viewing across those titles is reported at
2.81 billion viewings. A form that started on a phone is now scheduled against
a channel's evening, which is a scaling problem of exactly the kind this slide
is about.
```
