## Weaker control, faster answers

Nothing anchors composition but the prompt. That is less control than
starting from a keyframe, and the fastest way to test an idea before
committing an image to it.

```notes
That's weaker control than starting from a keyframe, but it's the fastest way
to test an idea before committing an image to it, and every video generator
this course uses has a native clip length that the prompt has to respect
rather than fight.
```

## The native clip length

The duration one pass reliably holds together before coherence falls apart.
Write inside it rather than against it.

```notes
Every video generator this course uses has a native clip length — the
duration one pass reliably holds together before coherence starts to fall
apart — that the prompt has to respect rather than fight.
```

## The four sentences

Subject and action. Shot size. What the camera is doing. What the light is
doing. In that order, inside the clip length.

<LadderStill week={5} tier="t3" />

```notes
A usable video prompt collapses into four sentences, each doing one job: who
or what the subject is and what it's doing, how close the shot is — the shot
size, from wide to close-up — what the camera itself is doing while the
subject acts, and what the light is doing. Write those four in order, inside
the model's native clip length, and the prompt is usable before style ever
enters the conversation.
```

{/* _class: deck-claim--still */}

## Reading a bad take

Wrong physics, a quiet change of subject partway through, or a camera move
that contradicts the prompt. Each points at a different sentence.

<LadderStill week={5} tier="t5" />

```notes
A failed text-to-video take tends to fail in one of three specific ways:
motion that reads as physically wrong rather than merely stylised, a cut
where the model quietly changes what the subject looks like partway through
the clip, or a camera move that contradicts what the prompt asked for. Naming
which of the three happened is more useful than a general "this doesn't look
right," because each one points at a different sentence in the prompt to fix.
```

## The ladder

1. One line naming only the subject
2. Plus the subject's action
3. The full four sentences — subject, shot size, camera, light
4. Plus a specific beat the action turns on, and a negative prompt

And a counter-example, not a fifth rung: the same four sentences buried under
contradictory camera and style demands, generated once on purpose.

```notes
The ladder for a text-to-video prompt runs four rising tiers, plus one tier
that is not a top but a warning. The lowest tier is one line naming only the
subject. The next tier adds the subject's action. The next tier writes out
the full four sentences — subject, shot size, camera, light. The top tier
keeps those four and adds a specific beat the action turns on, plus a
negative prompt naming what the shot must not do. And a counter-example, not
a fifth rung: the same four sentences buried under contradictory camera and
style demands at once, which reliably produces a worse result than the plain
four sentences alone — worth generating once, on purpose, to see what
over-specifying actually costs.
```
